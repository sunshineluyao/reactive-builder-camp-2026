# Three starting compositions

**English** · [简体中文](layout-recipes.zh-CN.md)

| Reader's question | Composition | Detail that makes it credible |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What changes when a rule fires? | Show two observed inputs on a common scale. Label the decision boundary and put the recorded outcome at the end. | Units, boundary inclusion or exclusion, and a receipt, specimen or log readers can inspect. |
| How is this system organized? | Place the incoming object at one side, give the distinctive operation the largest region, and reserve the final region for the party that controls or verifies the result. | A clear distinction between proposed components and the portion already demonstrated. |
| What can someone learn from this collection or comparison? | Enlarge one real specimen or pair of measurements. Annotate only the features needed to explain the claim. Put scope and provenance beside it. | Units, sample or release, observation period, and the source of the example. |

## A small design system

- Establish the reading order with scale, weight, position and empty space. Avoid giving every component an equally prominent card. A diagram can use one shared field.
- Keep a large outer margin, a smaller consistent internal inset, and free gutters for arrows or labels. Use an alignment line shared by headings, marks and evidence. Thumbnail the composition and check which element you see first.
- Choose one base ink, a raised surface, strong foreground text, quiet secondary text and up to three semantic accents. Compare text contrast against the actual fill behind it. Aim for 4.5:1 for ordinary text and 3:1 for large text or meaningful graphical marks. Use direct wording, solid versus open marks, or shape differences so color is never the only signal.
- Draw a mark that belongs to the subject. A peg band, a specimen silhouette, a sensor footprint or a paired schedule conveys more than a generic decorative icon. Label hypothetical diagrams and illustrative data wherever the difference matters.
- For a phone, change the reading direction, shorten line lengths and retain the one limitation needed to interpret the result. An adjacent text link lets readers open the narrow figure independently of the host.

## A three-glance review

1. At thumbnail size, can a new reader identify the subject and focal point?
2. At normal desktop and phone widths, can they follow the evidence or decision and read its units?
3. After following the nearby link, can they verify what was observed and distinguish it from what the project aims to do?

## Color and illustration at working scale

For a jewel-toned technical cover, try near-black plum `#100D20` for the field and lifted plum `#261937` for the central surface. Give evidence marks a clear teal `#47E6D2`, the middle decision a lilac `#C49AFF`, and the consequential result a small amber `#FFCA69`. Use soft white `#F5F1FB` for ordinary text. The accents work because relatively little of the canvas uses them. Tint a panel's edge in its role color, then repeat that hue at the relevant mark, connector or proof. One soft pool of light behind the mechanism can give the scene depth. Keep continuous bands or data curves tied to real measurements.

For an archival or natural-history subject, the same roles can be carried by a warm paper field, deep ink, a cool annotation and a single bright specimen mark. Choose colors against the actual subject and test contrast for that surface. Start with a small silhouette that a reader in the domain would recognize. A few precise lines that reveal how the object works give it more identity than several generic interface icons.
